Students Have Been Serving Communities. What If Communities Taught Them Instead?

Comprenticeship reframes service learning as a two-way apprenticeship, where the community is the teacher and the student is the apprentice.

A proposal for a new first-year course · UNIV 1015

For decades, colleges have asked students to "give back." Students sign up for a shift, log their hours, and move on. The hours get counted. The neighborhood gets a few extra hands for an afternoon. And then everyone goes home.

It is well meant, but it often runs one way. The student arrives as the helper, and the community is cast as the one being helped. The people who know the neighborhood best, the organizers, elders, staff and residents who have kept it going for years, rarely get to be the experts in the room.

Comprenticeship turns that around. It borrows from one of the oldest ways people have learned anything: the apprenticeship. An apprentice works beside someone who knows the craft, over time, with growing responsibility. In comprenticeship, the craft is the community itself, and the community is the teacher.

Learning with the community, not for it.

The idea builds on the established field of community-engaged learning, with three commitments:

  • The community is an expert source of knowledge. Students come to learn, not to fix.
  • The relationship is a weekly routine over a full semester. Trust takes time, so one-off visits are not enough.
  • The final project is shaped by what the partner asks for. Not by what a student assumes is needed.

In practice, students spend a semester with one community partner, ideally in a small group of two or three classmates so they build belonging with each other as well as with the community. Students can also choose to go solo. They listen before they act. They show up every week. And they finish by co-creating something the partner actually asked for, then celebrate it together, with partners in the room as honored co-educators.

The result is a different kind of graduate: one who has learned that showing up, again and again, is how you become part of a place.

Word of the Semester

com·pren·tice

noun

Say it: kom-PREN-tiss

The practice: comprenticeship · kom-PREN-tiss-ship

A student who learns with a community, not for it, through a semester-long apprenticeship in which the community is the teacher.

Etymology

Origin of the Word

com-Latin

A prefix meaning "together" or "with," as in companion, compassion and community.

prenticeMiddle English

The old short form of "apprentice," which traces back to the French apprendre, "to learn."

communisLatin

The root of "community": com- (together) plus munus, a shared gift or duty. The word for community already meant giving to each other.

The two-way relationship was in the word all along.

What's Different

Service Learning vs. Comprenticeship

The biggest change is the direction of the relationship.

The expertThe student or the schoolThe community
TimeOften one-off shifts or scattered hours18 hours of weekly routine with one partner over a semester

Partner's roleHost siteCo-educator, credited and honored

Many strong service-learning programs already include reflection and reciprocity. Comprenticeship makes them the default.

For Community Organizations

Community Partners Are Co-Educators

Your organization's knowledge is the curriculum. Comprenticeship is built so that hosting students strengthens your work instead of adding to it.

  • Welcome students, share your knowledge, and name a real project need.
  • Host 2 to 5 students per semester, usually one or two small groups, so you are never overwhelmed.
  • Receive public recognition as a co-educator in course materials and at the final showcase.
  • Give feedback twice a semester; faculty handle all grades.

    Learning Outcomes

    By the end of the semester, students will be able to:

    1. Explain the difference between one-way service and reciprocal, community-engaged learning.
    2. Map a community's strengths, cultural assets, history and people, not only its needs.
    3. Listen to and learn from community members as teachers and experts, without imposing solutions.
    4. Keep a consistent weekly commitment and build a trusted relationship over time.
    5. Co-create a project with a community partner, based on what the partner asks for.
    6. Reflect on how the community changed their thinking, values, civic identity and sense of belonging.
    7. Tell the story of their experience honestly and with consent, crediting the community's leadership.

    Enrollment & Opt-Out Policy
    • Pilot phase: launches as an elective first-year seminar, with sections sized to the number of partners who have agreed to host.
    • Default registration after the pilot: once partner capacity is confirmed, incoming first-year students are pre-registered.
    • Simple opt-out: one step in the registration portal before the add/drop deadline. No advisor meeting required.
    • Alternative pathways: students who work, commute or care for family can complete the placement through a campus-based partner project or community work they already do.
    • Scaling rule: new sections open only when enough partners can host, so no organization is asked to take more students than it can teach.
    The Comprentice Certification

    Students who complete the course earn the Comprentice Certification: Reciprocal Community Impact, a stackable digital credential that can be shared on LinkedIn and added to the co-curricular record.

    To earn it, a student must:

    1. Complete all 8 modules and 15 seminar meetings.
    2. Earn a final grade of B or higher.
    3. Verify 18 field hours with their partner (missed visits can be made up).
    4. Receive a satisfactory final evaluation from their partner.
    5. Deliver the project the partner requested.
    6. Give a consent-based showcase presentation that credits community co-educators.

    Appeals: if a placement ends for reasons outside a student's control, or a student disagrees with a partner evaluation, the instructor reviews the evidence and may arrange a new placement.
